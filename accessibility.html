Accessibility & Language Access

Accessibility here is architectural, not decorative. This site is designed to WCAG 2.2 Level AA as a minimum baseline, an independent conformance audit has not yet been completed, and access features are never — under any future business model — a premium upgrade. This page states the full standard and the honest distance between the standard and what has been verified.

Why this page existsAccessibility as observable dignity

This framework claims that every male person — and every person — holds dignity that is not earned by health, independence, or performance. A website can say that, or it can demonstrate it. Accessibility is where the claim becomes observable: either a blind visitor, a Deaf visitor, a visitor with tremor, a visitor in cognitive overload, or a visitor reading in a second language can actually use the framework, the care and crisis pathways, and the reflection tools — or the dignity language was decoration.

The person is not an interruption to the design. Accessibility is one of the ways dignity becomes observable.

Never a premium feature

Accessibility features must never be a premium upgrade. No one should ever need a higher membership tier to understand the service, use safety information, communicate, cancel, request a refund, or protect their privacy. Accessibility features, cancellation, refunds, safety information, privacy controls, and crisis pathways must never be premium features — a rule that binds every current page and every future paid service on the membership model.

This commitment tightened over the project’s own correction process: earlier accessibility commitments were broad statements of intent. The governing formulation is now specific — WCAG 2.2 Level AA as the minimum technical baseline, manual testing, disabled-user governance, multilingual architecture, and no paywall on access features.

The standardThe technical baseline: WCAG 2.2 Level AA

WCAG 2.2 Level AA is the minimum technical baseline for every part of this platform — public pages today, and accounts, subscriptions, journals, check-ins, video, community, payment, cancellation, refund, and professional-care systems if and when those launch (none of the account, payment, community, or clinical systems is currently live; the baseline is a precondition of their launch, enforced through launch Gate C). In October 2025, WCAG 2.2 was approved as ISO/IEC 40500:2025, strengthening its international institutional standing. [R20]

WCAG 2.2 addresses blindness, low vision, deafness, hearing loss, limited movement, speech disabilities, photosensitivity, combinations of disabilities, and some cognitive and learning needs — while acknowledging that no standard covers every user need. [R20] Compliance is therefore a baseline, not proof that every person can use the service. Conformance testing must be combined with manual testing and paid evaluation by disabled users throughout research, design, development, launch, and ongoing review: W3C’s own guidance emphasizes that real users identify barriers that technical conformance checks alone do not discover. [R20]

The populations this platform must support, by explicit commitment: blind and low-vision users, Deaf and hard-of-hearing users, DeafBlind users, people with motor and speech disabilities, people with cognitive and learning access needs, neurodivergent people, people with psychiatric disability, people with photosensitivity, people with vestibular conditions, aging users, and multilingual participants. On why disability never diminishes dignity or defines a person, see Disability & capacity.

CommitmentsAccess domains and required product behavior

The standard is stated per access domain, as required product behavior rather than aspiration:

Access domains and the product behavior each requires
Access domainRequired product behavior
Blind and low-vision accessSemantic structure, screen-reader support, meaningful alt text, long descriptions for diagrams, keyboard navigation, zoom and reflow, high contrast, audio description, text equivalents for infographics.
Deaf and hard-of-hearing accessAccurate captions, transcripts, live captioning, visual alerts, text support, qualified sign-language interpretation where appropriate, no audio-only essential information.
DeafBlind accessRefreshable Braille compatibility, tactile and qualified communication support, accessible text, no dependence on audio or vision alone.
Motor accessKeyboard, switch control, speech input, large targets, no drag-only tasks, saved progress, flexible time, alternatives to biometrics and handwriting.
Speech and communication accessText, secure messaging, relay, AAC, speech-to-speech support, additional time, and direct communication with the man himself rather than only with a companion.
Cognitive and neurological accessPredictable navigation, plain-language summaries, low-stimulation mode, clear steps, visible progress, saved state, extended time, consistent terminology, and recovery from errors.
Psychological and emotional accessContent notes, pause controls, no shame-based streaks, no forced disclosure, no surprise alarms, topic controls, reduced notifications, and easy access to support.
Multilingual accessInternationalized architecture, correct language tags, right-to-left support, localized dates and currency, human-reviewed critical translations, and equivalent functionality across languages.

How to meet a particular person’s need is not decided by category. ADA effective-communication guidance emphasizes that the appropriate aid depends on the nature, length, complexity, and context of the communication and the person’s normal method of communicating — and may include qualified interpreters, captioning, accessible electronic material, screen readers, speech-to-speech support, and additional time. The person, not the platform, is the authority on what works; the same principle that gives each man control of his own disability terminology.

Honest statusWhat this site does today

Current conformance status, stated plainly

This site is designed to WCAG 2.2 Level AA. An independent accessibility audit has not yet been completed, and the project’s July 22, 2026 dossier lists a full assistive-technology audit among the formal tests still outstanding. What follows are implementation strengths, not a complete conformance determination — and launch Gate C blocks any claim of conformance until the full test matrix below has been passed and an accessibility statement reports scope, contact, and known limitations honestly.

The current shipped experience demonstrates deliberate accessibility intent:

  • a skip-to-content link on every page;
  • visible focus treatments on interactive controls;
  • a single first-level heading on each page, with a generally logical second- and third-level heading structure;
  • keyboard-operable dialog tabs and controls in the well-being check-in;
  • focus containment within open dialogs, Escape-key closure, and focus restoration when a dialog closes;
  • dark-mode styling with a persistent user toggle;
  • responsive breakpoints for desktop, tablet, and narrow mobile widths;
  • a reduced-motion mode honoring the operating-system preference, plus a higher-contrast media preference;
  • ambient background motion that is noninteractive, does not intercept pointer input, and is coded for exclusion from the accessibility tree (with a caveat described below);
  • an explicit on-site Calm mode control, available in the header of every page, that turns the moving background words off entirely and remembers the choice locally;
  • and plain-language privacy disclosures.

One caution from the project’s own audit is preserved here deliberately: code intended to exclude the animated ambient words from the accessibility tree produced conflicting accessibility-snapshot evidence on the audited release, so effective hiding from screen readers requires manual verification with real assistive technology, not inference from source code.

VerificationThe formal test matrix still required

Before WCAG 2.2 AA conformance may be claimed, the following must be tested at minimum:

Limit

Testing must include the entire consent and deletion flow of the check-in, not just static pages. A keyboard-accessible button does not prove that consent is comprehensible or that destructive actions are safely reversible.

A specific scrutinyAmbient motion and the calm-mode control

The oversized drifting “Dignity / Integrity / Sovereignty” background language gives the site a living editorial atmosphere. It is marked hidden from assistive technology, pointer-inert, slow, responsive, and static or disabled under reduced motion — yet a separate accessibility snapshot of the audited release nevertheless exposed the repeated words, so the effective assistive-technology result remains unresolved until verified with real screen readers.

The remaining risks are recorded rather than minimized:

  • outline text may cross behind body copy differently on devices not represented in the original screenshots;
  • dark-mode opacity and compositing may reduce local contrast;
  • long-duration movement can distract people who have not set an operating-system reduced-motion preference;
  • users with attention or vestibular sensitivities may prefer an explicit on-site pause;
  • and the animation may increase GPU or battery use on low-power mobile devices.

The required control set: central opacity and stroke tokens tested against every surface; an optional “pause background motion” control stored locally; no vertical parallax or rapid acceleration; pausing when the document is hidden; a mobile performance budget; and screenshot and contrast regression tests in both themes. The locally stored pause control exists on this site today: the Calm mode button in the header of every page turns the moving background words off and keeps them off across visits on the same browser.

Comprehension is accessReading burden and cognitive accessibility

This site is intentionally a long argument. That depth is philosophically appropriate — and density can still become an accessibility barrier for visitors who are distressed, fatigued, cognitively overloaded, on a phone, or looking for one immediate action. The solution is layered entry, not deletion of substance:

  • a concise start page stating the thesis with direct actions;
  • a six-proposition framework summary readable in minutes;
  • a compact map of the framework with estimated reading time;
  • “read the full argument” chapters for those who want the complete case;
  • direct routes to the check-in, care resources, privacy, evidence, and governance;
  • and a plain-language summary maintained alongside the full doctrine.
Core standard

No essential crisis, privacy, consent, or access information may ever be reachable only through the long-form argument. Crisis resources appear on every page of this site, outside the prose, with no reading prerequisite.

LocalizationLanguage, literacy, and localization

Multilingual access is part of the accessibility standard, not a marketing expansion. Future language expansion must not use machine translation as an unreviewed substitute for professional localization — above all for crisis, clinical, consent, and legal content. A localization release requires:

  • a defined locale and jurisdiction;
  • qualified translation or qualified review;
  • cultural adaptation rather than word substitution;
  • translated privacy and consent language at equivalent readability;
  • local emergency and care resources;
  • screen-reader and layout testing in the target language;
  • and a visible last-reviewed date.

Until localized resources are verified, the current United States focus of the crisis and care pathways remains explicit rather than implied. Telling a visitor honestly that resources are U.S.-scoped is an access feature; letting them assume otherwise is not.

The checklistThe accessibility non-negotiables

These conditions are pre-launch non-negotiables for the platform as a whole. They are commitments to be verified, not achievements being claimed:

  • WCAG 2.2 Level AA minimum. [R20]
  • Manual screen-reader, keyboard, zoom, captions, transcripts, contrast, reduced-motion, and cognitive-access testing.
  • Paid disabled-user advisory participation across research, design, development, launch, and ongoing review.
  • Accessible billing, cancellation, refund, privacy, and crisis pathways.
  • Human-reviewed safety, consent, clinical, privacy, and billing translations.
  • Right-to-left support, language metadata, and text expansion.
  • No accessibility paywall — ever.
  • A barrier-reporting and alternative-format pathway, so that anyone who cannot use part of the service can say so and receive an equivalent.

Accessibility conformance does not eliminate the need for user testing and individual accommodation. Progress against these commitments is governed by launch Gate C, and any correction to this standard is recorded in the public corrections log.
