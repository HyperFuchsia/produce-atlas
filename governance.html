Permanent constitutional ruleContinuous scrutiny; no autonomous authority

Continuous scrutiny; no autonomous authority.

That is the governing phrase for every present and future automation in this project. “Self-governing” must never mean that a model determines morality, rewrites the website, silently changes safeguards, or treats its own output as consent. In this project, the legitimate meaning is narrower: the review process continually checks itself and the publication against explicit standards, records uncertainty, identifies possible failures, and brings structured proposals to an accountable human decision-maker.

What AI may eventually be permitted to do

  • Read approved public content and approved source files.
  • Identify contradictory, unclear, unsupported, outdated, discriminatory, stigmatizing, unsafe, inaccessible, or privacy-invasive material.
  • Classify the type and possible severity of a concern.
  • Retrieve candidate evidence and counterevidence for manual verification.
  • Explain the risk of leaving a passage unchanged.
  • Explain the risk of revising it incorrectly.
  • Draft one or more proposed revisions.
  • Indicate when legal, clinical, crisis, privacy, security, safeguarding, or accessibility expertise is required.
  • Compare a proposed change with the framework’s public principles.
  • Create a private owner note.

What AI must never be permitted to do

  • Publish, deploy, merge, delete, revert, enroll, bill, refund, message, or change policy.
  • Alter crisis information, medical boundaries, privacy terms, security rules, or owner access.
  • Access check-ins, journals, crisis disclosures, payment information, clinical records, identities, or other visitor data.
  • Infer morality, culpability, diagnosis, masculinity, or decisional capacity from a person’s health or writing.
  • Treat silence, prior approval, urgency, model confidence, or the owner’s continued use as consent.
  • Fabricate citations or represent an unverified source as established evidence.
  • Hide disagreement, counterevidence, uncertainty, or the possibility that the model’s concern is mistaken.
  • Describe a drafted proposal as “approved,” “safe,” “ethical,” or “published.”
Enforced by architecture, not by prompt

These are not prompt-only preferences. Before activation they must be enforced by architecture: read-only credentials, no repository-write token, no deployment token, no billing authority, no production database access, no private-user-data path, schema validation, immutable audit events, explicit approval states, and a separate controlled release process.

Assistive uses and hard boundaries

Within those limits, AI may assist with navigation, summarization, organizing reflections, accessibility, and drafting questions for human professionals. It must not independently diagnose mental illness or abuse, determine legal fault or incapacity, predict suicide or violence with certainty, replace crisis support, advise medication cessation, prescribe confrontation, decide whether a relationship should end, or assign masculinity, dignity, or moral-worth scores.

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework and its generative-AI profile provide useful lifecycle structures for governing risks to individuals, organizations, and society. Whole Man commits to implementing human override, model-version records, red-team testing, accessibility testing, bias testing, crisis-failure testing, incident review, user correction, and opt-out.[R21]

Human authorityOwner authority is necessary but not always sufficient

The owner — identified for the project through a single configured owner identity, never published on this site — retains final executive authority over website publication. No executive change should occur without the owner’s explicit, change-specific approval.

That rule protects sovereignty, but ethical governance must also resist the proposition that owner preference alone makes a high-risk claim safe. The owner voluntarily imposes an additional launch gate for material involving:

  • diagnosis, treatment, clinical interpretation, or medication;
  • suicide, self-harm, abuse, violence, immediate danger, or emergency action;
  • privacy rights, consent, data collection, retention, deletion, or security;
  • child or adolescent participation and safeguarding;
  • disability, decisional capacity, or supported decision-making;
  • discrimination, civil rights, employment, benefits, or other legal rights;
  • professional licensure, provider scope, supervision, or clinical records;
  • accessibility conformance claims;
  • payments, refunds, advertising, guarantees, or consumer representations;
  • and any statement that could materially alter a visitor’s health, safety, rights, or financial decision.
The rule for high-risk categories

Owner approval is required, and qualified human review is a prerequisite the owner chooses not to waive.

The specialist does not acquire control of the website. The specialist provides evidence within a defined scope; the owner remains the executive decision-maker and records the final rationale.

Structured concern, not vague warningThe required Governance Inbox note

Every generated concern must be presented as a structured critical memorandum, not a vague warning. A complete note includes:

  1. Note identifier and immutable creation time.
  2. Exact passage or behavior. Quote only the minimum necessary and identify route, section, version, and commit.
  3. Concern category. For example: factual accuracy, evidence, clinical safety, crisis safety, privacy, security, accessibility, legal, consumer protection, discrimination, philosophical coherence, scope, tone, or implementation mismatch.
  4. Severity and urgency. Use defined criteria, not model mood.
  5. People potentially affected. Include visitors, survivors, disabled people, minors, prospective customers, providers, moderators, and the owner where relevant.
  6. Principle implicated. Point to an explicit published standard.
  7. Argument supporting the concern. State the causal chain and assumptions.
  8. Evidence for the concern. Provide verified sources, evidence type, population, date, and limitations.
  9. Counterargument or benign interpretation. The system must try to disconfirm its own conclusion.
  10. Uncertainty and missing facts. State what is not known.
  11. Risk if unchanged. Separate likelihood, severity, reversibility, and exposure.
  12. Risk if changed incorrectly. Prevent “fixes” that create new harm.
  13. Recommended revision. Provide the smallest viable change.
  14. Alternatives. Include no-change-with-monitoring where reasonable.
  15. Specialist review requirement. Identify expertise without pretending the model has it.
  16. Validation plan. Specify tests, pages, devices, identities, and acceptance criteria.
  17. Rollback plan. Identify the exact prior version and trigger.
  18. AI confidence and known limitations. Confidence must never substitute for evidence.
  19. Owner decision. Approve, reject, revise, defer, request evidence, or close as not applicable.
  20. Decision rationale and resulting release record. Preserve an audit trail.
Notes never deploy

No note status should itself deploy anything. “Approved” means permission to prepare a controlled release; it is not proof that the resulting code or wording is correct. The proposed artifact must still be reviewed, built, tested, and separately released.

A stable checklistEthical review criteria

The model and human reviewers apply a stable checklist to each substantive change:

  1. Does it preserve equal and inherent dignity?
  2. Does it distinguish identity from conduct?
  3. Does it preserve protection and accountability simultaneously?
  4. Does it avoid presuming guilt, innocence, morality, or incapacity from group membership or diagnosis?
  5. Does it identify affected people and power asymmetries?
  6. Does it protect survivor choice and refuse compelled reconciliation?
  7. Does it preserve disability dignity and supported agency?
  8. Does it label normative judgment, empirical evidence, inference, and uncertainty distinctly?
  9. Does it use the least restrictive, least data-intensive, reversible mechanism?
  10. Does it avoid manipulating visitors through shame, fear, dependency, artificial urgency, or masculinity qualification?
  11. Does it keep marketing promises within actual service capacity?
  12. Does it preserve public access to crisis and foundational material?
  13. Does it have an accountable owner, evidence record, test, and rollback path?

“Moral” and “ethical” should not become free-floating labels. A concern must name the principle, the affected party, the evidence, the mechanism, the proportional response, and a reasonable counterinterpretation.

Correct future production-key procedure

When the reviewer is ready — not before — the safest procedure is:

  1. Confirm the OpenAI Platform account and organization belonging to the intended owner.
  2. Create a dedicated production project with billing alerts and a conservative hard operational budget where the platform supports it.
  3. Create a service-account or project key restricted to that project; do not use an organization admin key.
  4. Name the credential unambiguously — for example, Whole Man Production Governance.
  5. Capture the secret exactly once through the secure creation flow.
  6. Store it directly in the production host’s protected server-side secret manager as OPENAI_API_KEY.
  7. Never place it in a variable exposed to client bundles, including any PUBLIC_ or NEXT_PUBLIC_ convention.
  8. Do not paste it into chat, email, a screenshot, analytics, logging output, an issue tracker, or a repository.
  9. Configure the approved model separately.
  10. Run a minimal server-side connectivity test that sends no private visitor information.
  11. Verify request logging, redaction, timeout, budget, and failure behavior.
  12. Confirm through built assets and browser developer tools that the secret is never shipped to the browser.
  13. Activate only a read-only review job with no write, deployment, or private-data credential.
  14. Test note creation without publication.
  15. Record creation date, owner, project, last four characters or key identifier, permitted use, rotation date, and revocation procedure — never the full secret.
Four separate decisions

Creation, installation, reviewer activation, and public deployment are four separate decisions. Authorization for one must not be stretched into authorization for the next.

Access-control acceptance tests

The owner route will not be called fully verified until all of the following pass:

  • A signed-out visitor is redirected to authentication and cannot see owner content.
  • A signed-in user whose verified email is not the allowlisted address receives no owner content.
  • The allowlisted, verified owner can enter.
  • Email comparison is normalized safely and derives only from a trusted identity-provider claim.
  • Changing a browser-visible field or request parameter cannot impersonate the owner.
  • Session expiration removes access.
  • Sign-out removes access.
  • Browser back/forward cache does not reveal private content after sign-out.
  • Owner responses are not stored in public or shared caches.
  • Direct API endpoints repeat authorization checks instead of trusting the page route.
  • Failed access attempts are recorded without storing unnecessary identity data.
  • Recovery requires a documented, owner-approved identity change rather than a bypass code.
  • A locked-out owner can recover through the identity provider without exposing API secrets.

The owner’s email must not be rendered into public HTML, client JavaScript, error text, or public source. If the framework currently injects it into code for a server-side comparison, the built output must be inspected to confirm it is not unnecessarily exposed.

Secret incident response

If an API key is ever shown in chat, email, logs, browser code, a screenshot, or a repository, it is treated as compromised even if no misuse is visible:

  1. Revoke the exposed key immediately.
  2. Create a replacement only after identifying the exposure path.
  3. Update the protected production secret.
  4. Redeploy or restart only the server component that reads it.
  5. Inspect usage and audit records for unexplained calls.
  6. Preserve enough incident evidence to understand the event without preserving the secret itself.
  7. Verify that public bundles and repository history contain no copy.
  8. Document prevention changes and owner approval.

Never ask anyone to post a complete key in a conversation “just once.” Secure systems are designed so operators can configure a secret without other participants viewing it.

Current acceptance criteriaLaunch gates A–G

No capability advances without passing its gate. These are the current acceptance criteria, in force as of the July 22, 2026 audit; the fuller institutional audit below sets the deeper standard behind them. See also the prioritized roadmap.

Gate A — Editorial integrity

Pass only if: central definitions are public and consistently used; intrinsic worth precedes social usefulness; “whole” is explicitly non-perfectionist; health is separated from morality and diagnosis from capacity; survivor-safety and anti-scapegoating standards are public; empirical claims have sources, dates, scope, and limitations; normative claims are labeled as ethical commitments; and current/planned/offline status language is accurate on every route.

Gate B — Public-tool safety

Pass only if: the complete check-in works in all four storage modes; skip, stop, preview, and topic choice work; no score, diagnosis, or safety clearance is produced; no-save is genuinely available; edit, single delete, delete all, erase everything, and export work; exported files carry a clear unencrypted-file warning; browser/device limits are visible; storage failure and corruption fail safely; crisis resources remain reachable without completing the tool; and synthetic test data is removed.

Gate C — Accessibility

Pass only if: critical automated issues are zero; initial modal focus is corrected; keyboard paths and focus return work; screen-reader testing succeeds; 200%/400% zoom and 320-pixel reflow work; light/dark contrast and focus indicators pass; reduced motion and optional motion pause work; touch targets and mobile safe areas work; and an accessibility statement reports scope, contact, and known limitations honestly.

Gate D — Privacy and security

Pass only if: current data flows are documented and match public wording; no private answer is sent to the server in the current architecture; owner content is not publicly cached or exposed; authorization passes signed-out, owner, and non-owner tests; no secret or owner identifier leaks into public assets unnecessarily; security headers and dependencies are reviewed; backups, logs, and incident procedures are defined; and any new server-side data feature receives a new privacy assessment before launch.

Gate E — Governance activation

Pass only if: the AI reviewer has a read-only input scope; it has no publish, write, deployment, billing, or visitor-data authority; the service credential is protected and budgeted; every note includes evidence, counterargument, uncertainty, and rollback thinking; owner approval changes no public state automatically; qualified-review categories are enforced; audit history is immutable enough for accountability; outage and injection tests fail safely; and the live public status is updated only after evidence of actual operation.

Gate F — Paid service

Pass only if: the service is available in the stated geography and volume; every benefit has an owner and a delivery procedure; prices cover ethical staffing and support; billing, cancellation, refunds, complaints, and chargebacks are tested; marketing does not exploit distress; basic crisis, privacy, and foundational resources remain free; and the service can close enrollment before it overpromises. See Membership & services for the current honest status: paid enrollment is closed.

Gate G — Clinical or community service

Pass only after a separate qualified review confirms professional governance, licensing and scope, records, insurance, emergency response, safeguarding, moderation, staffing, accessibility, and operational resilience. A technically functional feature is not enough.

Compact standardQuality & launch checklist

The compact quality standard every launch must satisfy:

  • Every core claim has a source, qualification, or clear label as ethical argument or project terminology.
  • No tool diagnoses, ranks masculinity, or determines legal fault.
  • Every paid feature answers what the user can understand, decide, communicate, change, repair, document, or access because it exists.

  • Moderation distinguishes help-seeking from advocacy of harm.
  • Editorial corrections, review dates, and evidence standards are public.
  • No content manufactures male inadequacy or demands masculine proof.
  • No section protects males from accountability by degrading others.
  • No section protects accountability by degrading male identity.

Appendix E, canonicalThe non-negotiable launch constitution

  13. Basic crisis, privacy, evidence, and foundational resources remain publicly accessible.
  14. Private reflection will never be coerced as a condition of dignity or access to basic help.
  15. The project will not diagnose, score masculinity, score dignity, or determine moral worth.
  16. Sensitive data collection will remain minimal and purpose-limited.
  17. High-risk claims receive qualified review.
  18. AI has no autonomous executive authority and no access to visitor check-ins or journals.
  19. Every material change requires explicit owner approval, testing, and rollback readiness.
  20. Corrections will be public, specific, and treated as evidence of integrity rather than failure.

It is complete only when the institution can demonstrate that its claims are evidence-disciplined, its language is proportionate, its safety systems are operational, its accessibility is tested, its data practices are restrained, its commercial model does not manufacture inadequacy, its professional roles are honest, its community resists grievance capture, and its own errors can be challenged and corrected.

The organization must protect males without flattering them, hold conduct accountable without pathologizing male existence, examine environments without erasing agency, support health without creating biological hierarchy, defend sovereignty without promoting domination, and recognize female and universal human integrity without allowing male-specific concerns to disappear.

The Whole Male Person. The Shared Human Future. Preserve dignity. Support biological life. Govern agency ethically. Examine the environment. Protect the harmed. Interrupt harmful conduct. Correct the institution when the institution fails.
