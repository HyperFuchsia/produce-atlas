## Concise definition

**lexopoet** *(n.)* — One who treats the growth of the lexicon as ongoing work rather
than a finished inheritance: who, on meeting a thing that has no name, refuses the
general word that merely gestures at it, hunts the term that fits exactly, coins one
when the hunt fails, and does this continuously as a standing practice.

Everything below is that sentence, taken apart.

---

## Sense tree

### 1. *(primary)* The standing practice

One whose settled disposition is the enlargement of the working vocabulary — who
treats the lexicon as **unfinished** and treats that as a call on their own time.

- **1a.** *(the persistence condition)* One who does this **continuously and
  indefinitely**, not once and not on commission. A person who coins a single word is a
  *neologist*; a lexopoet is characterised by not stopping.
- **1b.** *(the exactness condition)* One who will not accept a term that merely refers,
  and holds out for one whose boundary falls where the concept's does. See
  [The precision standard](#the-precision-standard).
- **1c.** *(scope)* One who does this **across every subject they touch**, rather than
  within a single professional vocabulary. The domain terminologist works one field by
  appointment; the lexopoet works whatever is in front of them, unappointed.

### 2. The prospector *(the gathering move)*

One who acquires words they do not yet have, for the sake of the **distinctions** those
words make available rather than for erudition or display. Vocabulary treated as
instrumentation.

- **2a.** From **other languages** — for concepts English does not force a speaker to
  mark: *saudade*, *ma* (間), *mamihlapinatapai*, *ostranenie*.
- **2b.** From **other disciplines** — the largest and most neglected reservoir, because
  the word already has a rigorous definition attached. *Hysteresis* sat fully formed in
  materials science for anyone needing to name why an effect outlasts its cause.
- **2c.** From **obsolete, dialectal, and regional English** — the language's own
  discards, many of which were dropped for fashion rather than redundancy.

### 3. The maker *(the building move)*

One who mints new terms where the search has failed, and — the part that distinguishes
this from mere coinage — **documents them to a standard that permits adoption**:
definition, etymology, boundary, and evidence of use.

- **3a.** *(minting)* Constructing the term from morphemes a listener can
  reverse-engineer.
- **3b.** *(specifying)* Fixing the boundary so that two people apply the term the same
  way. A coinage shipped without its boundary is a mood with a spelling.
- **3c.** *(occupational)* In a research group, standards body, or product organisation:
  the person accountable for the vocabulary itself — naming, disambiguating, and
  retiring dead terms. This sense alone corresponds to a recognised profession
  (see [ISO 704](#where-this-sits-in-the-existing-disciplines)).

### 4. *(transferred)* Of a body rather than a person

Of a publication, discipline, institution, or community that functions this way
collectively — that generates and maintains precise vocabulary as a matter of course.
*"Mycology is a lexopoet discipline; it has had to name ten thousand things nobody had
looked at closely before."* Rare; prefer the adjective **lexopoetic** in this use.

## The two conditions

The word rests on two. Neither is optional, and a person who fails either is something
else — the language already has words for those.

### The persistence is definitional

Sense 1 is not a summary of senses 2 and 3; it is the **condition that makes someone a
lexopoet at all**. Senses 2 and 3 are the two moves the practice alternates between,
governed by one rule:

> **Search first, mint on failure.** Prospecting costs less than coining, because a
> found word arrives with usage, connotation, and a community already attached to it.
> Roughly half of English is borrowed rather than made — the language has always run
> this way round.

The trigger is the same for both moves, and is what the word is *for*: **a thing with no
name.** An object, a problem, an abstract idea, or a distinction other people have not
marked and therefore cannot discuss.

### The fit must be exact

The condition that separates a lexopoet from an enthusiast. The target is never *a* word
for the wordless idea. It is the **acribonym** — the term whose boundary falls exactly
where the concept's does.

The failure mode is not silence; it is **the general word accepted too early**. Faced
with something unnamed, the ordinary move is to reach up a level and grab the nearest
superordinate — the hypernym, the umbrella, the catch-all — and this feels like success
because a sentence can now be finished. It is not. A hypernym names the genus and drops
the difference, and the dropped difference was the entire reason the thing needed a name.
*Bird* is no use to someone who meant *kestrel*.

> **A lexopoet would rather leave a thing unnamed for a year than name it
> approximately**, because an approximate name closes the file. Once a serviceable word
> exists, nobody looks for the exact one — the vague term squats on the gap and hides
> it. This is the most expensive mistake in the practice, and it is invisible while you
> are making it.

## Etymology

### Immediate formation

**λέξις** (*léxis*) "word, diction, manner of speaking" **+** **ποιητής** (*poiētḗs*)
"maker" — *a maker of words*.

That *poiētḗs* also gave English **poet** is not a collision to apologise for: *poet* is
the fossil of a word that meant, first and only, **maker**.

### First element: λέξις

λέξις is a verbal noun (*λεξ-* + *-σις*) from **λέγειν** (*légein*), whose earliest
sense is not "to speak" but **"to pick out, select, collect, enumerate"** — the sense of
speech developed from it, on the notion of *gathering words*, *picking words out*.

Ultimately from PIE **\*leǵ-** "to collect, gather."

| Language | Form | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| Ancient Greek | λέγειν, λέξις, λόγος | gather → speak; word; account, reason |
| Latin | *legere* | to gather, choose, pluck; **to read** |
| Latin | *lēx* | law — plausibly "a collection of rules" |
| Gothic | *lisan* | to collect, harvest |
| Lithuanian | *lèsti* | to pick |
| Albanian | *mb-ledh* | to collect, harvest |
| Hittite | *less-zi* | to pick, gather |

English descendants of the same root, via both branches: *collect, select, elect,
diligent, intelligent, legible, legend, lecture, legion, sacrilege, colleague* (Latin);
*logic, -logy, lexicon, dialect, eclectic, catalogue, prologue, analogy* (Greek).

### Second element: ποιητής

From **ποιεῖν** (*poieîn*) "to make," from PIE **\*kʷoiwo-** "making," from the root
**\*kʷei-** "to pile up, build, make." The Greek initial **p-** where the root shows
**kʷ-** is regular: Greek's treatment of the Indo-European labiovelars.

| Language | Form | Sense |
|---|---|---|
| Ancient Greek | ποιεῖν, ποιητής, ποίησις | to make; maker; a making |
| Sanskrit | *cinoti* | he heaps up, piles up |
| Old Church Slavonic | *činŭ* | act, deed, order, rank |

English descendants: *poet, poem, poesy, poetry, onomatopoeia, pharmacopoeia,
mythopoeia, logopoeia, autopoiesis, hematopoiesis, epopee*.

### The convergence

Reduced to their Proto-Indo-European roots, the two halves of **lexopoet** are:

> **\*leǵ-** *to gather* **+** **\*kʷei-** *to build*

**One who gathers and builds.** The word's deepest layer already encodes the two moves
of the practice and their order — sense 2 is the gathering, sense 3 is the building, and
the rule is that you gather before you build. This was not designed in. It fell out of
the roots, and it is the strongest single argument that the formation is the right one.

### Precedent for the formation

| Compound | Literal sense | English |
|---|---|---|
| ὀνοματοποιία *onomatopoiía* | name-making | **onomatopoeia** |
| μυθοποιία *mythopoiía* | myth-making | **mythopoeia**, **mythopoet**, **mythopoetic** |
| λογοποιία *logopoiía* | word-making | **logopoeia** — Ezra Pound's term for "the dance of the intellect among words" |
| φαρμακοποιία | drug-making | **pharmacopoeia** |
| αἱματοποίησις | blood-making | **hematopoiesis** |
| αὐτοποίησις | self-making | **autopoiesis** |

**mythopoet** is the exact structural model: a live English agent noun in `-poet`
meaning "one who makes myths." `lexopoet` is that word with *léxis* for *mŷthos*.

**logopoeia** is the closest semantic neighbour and worth distinguishing: Pound's term
names an *effect achieved in poetry by the play of word-associations*. It is a critical
term about literary technique. `lexopoiesis` names the *manufacture of the words
themselves*, outside any literary context.

### Why `-poiesis` carries the persistence

The reason the word is built this way and not another. In modern English the
`-poiesis` family does not mean *a making*. It means **continuous, self-sustaining
production**: *hematopoiesis* is the body never ceasing to make blood cells;
*autopoiesis* is a system perpetually producing itself. The morpheme has drifted, in
scientific usage, from *act* to *ongoing process* — which is precisely the load sense 1a
needs carried.

### Rejected formations

| Candidate | Reading | Why rejected |
|---|---|---|
| **lexiurge** | word-worker (λέξις + -ουργός, on *dramaturge*, and on **ὀνοματουργός** "name-maker" in Plato's *Cratylus*) | The first draft of this entry. `-ourgos` is **neutral about duration** — a word-worker might work once. Fails 1a. |
| **onomaturge** | name-maker | Direct transliteration of Plato's term. Means *names* only, and is already in use as the name of a Hebrew word-formation expert system. |
| **logophile** | word-lover | `-phile` names affection. Passive; fails 1, 2, and 3. |
| **lexicographer** | word-writer | Occupied, and describes the opposite discipline: records existing usage, professionally barred from inventing. |
| **lexicologist** | word-studier | `-logist` names knowledge *about* words, not production of them. |
| **\*lexilalia, \*neolexia** | — | `-lexia` is pathologised by *dyslexia*, *alexia*, *hyperlexia*; anything ending in it reads as a disorder. |
| **\*verbigen-** | word-producing | Blocked by **verbigeration**, an established psychiatric term for pathological repetition of words. |

## Pronunciation

**Preferred:** /ˈlɛk.səˌpoʊ.ɪt/ — **LEK**-suh-POH-it. Four syllables. Primary stress on
*lex-*, secondary on *-po-*, matching **mythopoet** exactly.

**Accepted variants:**
- /ˈlɛk.soʊˌpoʊ.ɪt/ — full *-o-* in the linking syllable; more formal, slightly
  laboured.
- /ˈlɛk.səˌpoʊ.ɛt/ — final syllable as *-et* rather than *-it*; regional.

**Not recommended:** /lɛkˈsɒp.ə.ɪt/ — stress dragged onto the second syllable, on the
false analogy of *monopoly*. It obscures the *lex-* element, which is the half a
listener needs to parse the word.

**Linking vowel.** The *-o-* is the Greek compositional vowel, as in *mytho-*,
*hemato-*, *phanero-*. Not *lexi-* here: `lexi-` is the combining form used before
consonants in Latin-mediated borrowings (*lexicography*), while the `-poiesis` family
takes `-o-` uniformly (*onomatopoeia*, *hematopoiesis*, *autopoiesis*).

**On the two process nouns.** `-poeia` and `-poiesis` are the same Greek root arriving by
different routes: `-poeia` through Latin in older borrowings (*onomatopoeia*,
*pharmacopoeia*), `-poiesis` through 19th- and 20th-century scientific coinage
(*hematopoiesis*, *autopoiesis*). The split is one of register, and this entry puts it to
work — **lexopoeia** for one act of making, **lexopoiesis** for the unbroken practice.

**Derivability check.** The paradigm above is not decoration. A term that cannot form its
verb, adjective, and adverb strands its speakers the moment they need to modify it, and
this kills more coinages than ugliness does. Stress throughout follows
*mythopoet / mythopoetic*, so a speaker who has that word derives the rest unaided.

**On acribonym.** From **ἀκριβής** (*akribḗs*) "exact, precise, accurate" + **ὄνομα**
(*ónoma*) "name," on the pattern of *synonym*, *hypernym*, *eponym*. The practice needed
a noun for its object: not "the right word," which begs the question, but *the term whose
extension coincides with the concept's*. Its opposite in use is the **hypernym**.

**On acribology.** The root ἀκριβής has already produced one English word in exactly this
territory — *acribology*, exactness in the use of language — so rare it appears in no
major dictionary. Per the method's own prospect-before-mint rule, an existing word
outranks a coinage: `acribology` is **adopted here rather than replaced**. Only the
agent-object noun, `acribonym`, had to be made. *(The Greek adjective's own etymology is
not securely established.)*

## Usage

**Register.** Neutral to approving. Not technical, not slang. Usable in general prose
with a gloss on first appearance.

**Grammar.** Countable. Takes the indefinite article. No capital, no hyphen, no italics
after first use.

**Attributive.** Prefer the adjective — *a lexopoetic habit*, *lexopoetic discipline* —
over the noun-pile *lexopoet habit*. Sense 4 tolerates the attributive noun but reads
better recast.

**Gloss on first use.** Elyot's own technique, and the entry's recommendation: introduce
the word with a familiar synonym or a short explanation beside it, so the reader is never
stopped. Elyot defended his coinages on exactly this ground — that each was "declared so
plainly … that no sentence is therby made derke or harde to understande."

**Common errors.**

1. **Using it for anyone who likes words.** That is *logophile*. The lexopoet
   **produces**.
2. **Using it for a single coinage.** That is *neologist*. Sense 1a is not decorative.
3. **Using it as a compliment for good prose.** That is *wordsmith* — a different craft,
   operating on sentences rather than on vocabulary.
4. **Applying it to someone who accepts approximate terms.** The exactness condition is
   constitutive; a prolific coiner of vague words is a *lexiphanes* with output.

### Illustrative citations

*Constructed for this entry. A coinage has no citation history; these show the senses in
running prose and are the minimum a new term must ship with.*

1. *(sense 1)* He is not a collector; he is a **lexopoet**. Every unnamed thing he runs
   into becomes an open file, and the file stays open until it has a word in it.
2. *(sense 1b)* "Burnout" is doing four jobs in that sentence and none of them well. What
   you want is an **acribonym** — something that excludes the three conditions you don't
   mean.
3. *(sense 2b)* A **lexopoet** reads other fields' glossaries the way a scavenger reads a
   scrapyard: not for the machine, for the parts.
4. *(sense 3c)* The standards committee has no **lexopoet**, which is why four teams
   describe one failure mode under four incompatible names and the incident log cannot
   be searched.
5. *(lexopoiesis)* Thirty minutes of **lexopoiesis** would have saved that project six
   months of arguing about whether the two teams disagreed or were describing different
   things.
6. *(adverbial)* English did not acquire its range by accident. It was talked into it,
   **lexopoetically**, over four centuries, by people who refused to accept that a
   thought had no word.
7. *(sense 4, transferred)* Taxonomy is a **lexopoetic** discipline by necessity: it
   cannot describe what it has not first agreed to call something.

## Synonymy

**lexopoet, neologist, logophile, lexicographer, wordsmith, terminologist, verbivore,
etymologist, lexiphanes** all concern a heightened relation to words, and are not
interchangeable.

A **neologist** coins. The word says nothing about why, how well, or how often; one
coinage, made in play and never repeated, qualifies. A **logophile** loves, which is a
disposition and not an activity. A **lexicographer** records what is already in use and
is *professionally barred from inventing* — the discipline's whole authority rests on
description. A **wordsmith** writes well, which is craft at the level of the sentence, not
of the vocabulary. A **terminologist** maintains one field's agreed vocabulary, usually
by appointment and usually defensively; the office is real and standardised, and it maps
onto sense 3c alone. A **verbivore** consumes. An **etymologist** looks backward. A
**lexiphanes** displays — the pejorative, and the lexopoet's characteristic failure mode
when the exactness condition slips and vocabulary becomes ornament.

**lexopoet** is the only one of the nine that requires all of: *the unnamed thing as
trigger*, *the search*, *the coinage on failure of the search*, *an exact rather than
approximate fit*, and *doing it continuously*. It is not a fancier word for any of the
others; it names the intersection none of them cover.

| Term | Names | Fails to name |
|---|---|---|
| **neologist** | coins new words | Persistence; the search half; any requirement of fit or need |
| **logophile** | loves words | Production of any kind |
| **lexicographer** | records existing usage | Invention — forbidden by the discipline |
| **wordsmith** | writes skilfully | Vocabulary as such; operates on sentences |
| **terminologist** | standardises a field's terms | Senses 1 and 2; works one field, by appointment |
| **verbivore** | devours words | Use, as against appetite |
| **etymologist** | traces origins | Everything forward-facing |
| **lexiphanes** | flaunts long words | Function — the term is pejorative by construction |
| **onomastician** | studies names | Common vocabulary; studies rather than makes |

**Antonyms.** *lexistasis* (the condition), *lexistatic* (of a person or institution).
No single established English noun names the person who actively refuses new terms;
*purist* is close but concerns correctness rather than inventory.

## The precision standard

### Accuracy and precision are two requirements, not one

Distinct in measurement theory, and they stay distinct in terminology, where they fail
differently and need different repairs.

- **Accuracy** — the term denotes **the concept you actually meant**. It fails when the
  word is sharp but aimed wrong: a clean, confident term that quietly imports a
  neighbouring concept and takes the discussion with it.
- **Precision** — the term's **boundary is sharp enough that two people apply it the same
  way**. It fails when the word is aimed correctly but has soft edges, so everyone agrees
  with it and no two people mean the same thing by it.

A term can be precise and inaccurate (rigorously naming the wrong concept), or accurate
and imprecise (pointed the right way, arbitrating nothing). A lexopoet needs both, and
the second is harder to notice, **because an imprecise term generates no disagreement —
which reads as consensus.**

### Precision is not narrowness

A precise term is one whose **boundary is sharp**, not one whose **extension is small**.
*Bird* is precise across ten thousand species; *thing-ish* is vague covering almost
nothing in particular. Narrowing a term does not sharpen it. Confusing the two produces
the practice's other characteristic failure: over-specified terms that cover one case,
the coiner's own, and die with it.

### The extension test

Run at method step 4, before attachment sets in. Take the candidate term and write down:

1. **Three things it must include.** If the term cannot comfortably take all three, it is
   too narrow — you have named an instance, not a concept.
2. **Three near misses it must exclude.** Not obvious non-cases: the *close* ones, the
   things a hasty reader would file under it. **This is the whole test.** If the term
   accepts any of the three, it is a hypernym in precise clothing, and the difference you
   were trying to name has already been dropped.
3. **One borderline case it genuinely cannot decide.** Every real term has exactly one
   *kind* of edge. Several unrelated kinds of borderline means the term is carrying two
   concepts and must be split into two terms.

A definition that survives this is an **acribonym**. A definition that cannot produce
list 2 was never a term — it was a mood.

### Where this sits in the existing disciplines

The practice is not freelance. It has a name, a literature, and a standard.

- **Onomasiology** runs *concept → designation*: starting from a thing to be named and
  asking what names it. Its inverse, **semasiology**, runs *word → meaning*. Nearly all
  everyday lookup is semasiological, which is why **a dictionary is the wrong instrument
  for this work and a thesaurus is worse** — a thesaurus answers "what else could I call
  the thing I can already name."
- **Extension and intension** are the working vocabulary of the boundary: the *extension*
  is everything the term applies to, the *intension* is the criteria deciding membership.
  Precision work is intension work.
- **Hypernym / hyponym** name the levels the practice moves between — genus and species.
  The discipline is *resisting upward drift* toward the hypernym.
- **Vagueness** (borderline cases, no sharp line) is not **ambiguity** (several discrete
  senses). They look alike in use and need opposite repairs: vagueness wants a stipulated
  boundary; ambiguity wants a split into two terms.
- **Semantic bleaching** explains where general terms come from. Precise words drift into
  vague ones through overuse — *awesome*, *literally*, *problematic*. The lexicon leaks
  precision continuously, which is the standing case for the practice: **lexopoiesis is
  not enrichment, it is maintenance.**
- **ISO 704**, the international standard for terminology work, codifies this for
  scientific, technical, industrial and administrative fields — governing how
  designations are formed and definitions formulated. Sense 3c is a recognised
  professional activity with a standard attached.

The literary tradition has its own name for the target: **le mot juste**, Flaubert's
principle that for any given thing there is one right word, and the writer's work is not
done until it is found. The lexopoet's amendment is the case Flaubert did not address —
*and when there is no right word, you make it.*

## The lexopoetic method

Steps 0–2 are the gathering; steps 3–8 are the building. It is a loop, not a procedure
you finish.

**0. Write the unfinishable sentence.** Name the gap by trying to say the thing and
failing. The stump of that sentence is the specification for the word you need. A gap you
cannot demonstrate is not a gap.

**1. Prove the gap is real.** If a clean two- or three-word phrase does the job, stop —
there is no gap, and a coinage would add only a lookup cost. Most felt gaps are impatience
with a phrase.

**2. Prospect before you mint.** Search in this order: other languages; adjacent technical
fields; obsolete and dialectal English. A found word arrives with usage already attached.

**3. Mint by morpheme, not by mouthfeel.** Choose roots a listener can reverse-engineer. A
word that must be memorised will not survive contact with people who did not coin it.

**4. Draw the boundary before you get attached to the word.** Run the
[extension test](#the-extension-test). A term you cannot draw a boundary for is not a
precise term you haven't finished defining — it is a hypernym you have grown fond of.

**5. Test the derivational family before committing.** Form the verb, adjective, adverb,
and abstract noun. *This kills more coinages than ugliness does.*

**6. Check the collision field.** Homophones, live technical senses in nearby fields,
pathologised suffixes, and — for anything that will travel — unfortunate readings in
other languages.

**7. Gloss it on first use.** Elyot's technique: the new word with its explanation beside
it, so the reader is never stopped.

**8. Ship it with a definition and citations.** A coinage without recorded usage is a
nonce word, and nonce words die with their author. Definition, etymology, boundary, three
uses in running prose: the minimum viable word.

Then return to step 0. The practice is the loop.

## The tradition

The word names nothing new, and that is the point: **it is how English got everything it
has.**

In the Tudor period English was widely held to be "rude" and "barbarous," lacking words
for learned ideas that Latin and Greek expressed easily. The response was a deliberate,
sustained campaign of coinage by writers later called the **Neologizers**, and a furious
backlash — the **Inkhorn Controversy** — from critics who found the new terms affected and
un-English. The Neologizers won in the only way such things are won: their words were
used.

**Sir Thomas Elyot** is the exemplar. In *The Boke Named the Gouernour* (1531) he
introduced Latin-derived terms because English had no equipment for the ideas he needed to
discuss, glossing each so readers could follow. Among the words he brought in:
*education*, *dedicate*, *maturity*, *participate* — and **persist**.

That last is worth sitting with. **The word you would reach for to describe this practice
was itself produced by this practice**, by a man doing exactly it, five centuries ago, and
attacked for doing it. Every one of those words is now invisible, ordinary English. That
is what success looks like here: not recognition, but absorption.

The line runs unbroken:

| | |
|---|---|
| **1531** | Elyot coins to fill gaps, and glosses each coinage — the method's step 7, five centuries early |
| **16th–17th c.** | The Inkhorn Controversy: the first sustained argument about whether the practice is legitimate |
| **c. 1590–1613** | Shakespeare, in whose printed work hundreds of words are first attested — first attestation reflects the survival of print as much as invention, but the scale is real |
| **18th–20th c.** | The compounding machinery of scientific Latin and Greek: the industrialisation of sense 3 |
| **1918–** | Pound's **logopoeia**; the Modernists treat vocabulary itself as material |
| **1931–** | Tolkien argues for **mythopoeia** as a serious act of making, against the charge that invented things are lies |
| **2009–** | John Koenig's *Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows* mints terms for named emotional gaps, each with an etymology — the full loop, in public, at volume |

## Field notes

Sense 2 in practice is not vocabulary-building, and the distinction matters. The target is
never a fancier synonym for a thought you already have. It is the word that carries a
**distinction English does not force you to make** — *ma* (間), the Japanese interval that
is a positive presence rather than an absence; *saudade*, a longing that is itself
pleasurable; *hysteresis*, borrowed sideways out of physics.

Each is a thought you can hold afterward and could not hold before. That is why the motive
belongs in the definition rather than in a usage note.

And it is why exactness is not fastidiousness here. A general word lets you **refer** to
the thing; only an exact one lets you **reason** about it — draw the inference, notice the
second case, find out you were wrong. A vague term cannot be falsified, so nothing built
on it can be tested.

> **Vocabulary is not a record of what you can say. It is the limit on what you can
> notice — and its precision is the limit on what you can then do with what you noticed.**
